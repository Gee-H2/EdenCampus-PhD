## Structure of this section

Seven streams are reviewed. Each subsection follows the same arc for auditability:
(i) what the stream studies; (ii) what it has established, with magnitudes;
(iii) its methodological character (scale realism, market realism, data
transparency per Section 3.5); and (iv) **its park-scale gap** — the closing
sentence that feeds Table 4 and assembles the synthesis. The recurring finding is
that each stream illuminates part of the business-park flexibility question but
none addresses the coordinated multi-vector estate as a market participant.

## 5.1 D1 — Commercial demand response

Commercial demand response (DR) studies the shifting or curtailment of
non-residential electrical load in response to price or grid signals. The stream
is mature for individual large loads (HVAC, refrigeration, data centres)
[REF], with reported demand-shift potentials of [FINDING: cite magnitude, e.g. %
of peak shiftable] in commercial buildings [REF]. **Park-scale gap:** DR studies
typically treat single buildings or single large loads, not the aggregated,
multi-tenant estate where the shiftable resource is distributed across meters and
governance is split — the coordination problem central to this review [REF].

## 5.2 D2 — Campus and estate microgrids

This stream examines microgrids at campus and estate scale — the closest existing
analogue to the business park. It has established the technical feasibility of
coordinated generation, storage, and controllable load behind a common boundary
[REF], with demonstrated solar self-consumption and peak-reduction results of
[FINDING: cite ranges] [REF]. **Park-scale gap:** the literature is dominated by
university and single-owner campuses (governance-coherent T3/T5 analogues) and by
technical optimisation that omits real market participation — it shows what a
coordinated estate *can do technically* but rarely how it participates in, or is
rewarded by, electricity markets [REF].

## 5.3 D3 — Energy communities

Energy-community research covers collective generation, storage, and demand
management under shared governance, including the EU renewable-energy-community
(REC) construct [REF]. It has developed governance models, cost-sharing
mechanisms, and evidence on member benefits [FINDING] [REF]. **Park-scale gap:**
the stream is oriented to residential and citizen communities and to jurisdictions
with an enabling REC legal form; it maps poorly onto the GB commercial estate,
which lacks both the REC legal basis (Section 2's RED II comparator) and the
citizen-participation model [REF].

## 5.4 D4 — Eco-industrial parks and industrial symbiosis

This stream studies material and energy exchange between co-located firms
(industrial symbiosis) and the eco-industrial-park concept [REF]. It has quantified
resource-efficiency and emissions benefits from inter-firm exchange [FINDING]
[REF]. **Park-scale gap:** the defining lens is material/energy *exchange between
firms*, not the estate's participation in *electricity markets* as a flexibility
provider; the electrical-flexibility dimension is largely absent, and the
governance analysed is bilateral exchange rather than estate-level coordination
for grid services [REF].

## 5.5 D5 — District-scale sector coupling

Sector-coupling research at district scale integrates electricity, heat, and
sometimes hydrogen/transport vectors [REF], establishing the value of
multi-vector co-optimisation for decarbonisation [FINDING] [REF]. This stream is
the closest to the multi-vector framing of the present review's empirical work.
**Park-scale gap:** studies are typically district- or city-scale and
planning-oriented, or single-building; the *business-park* scale — larger than a
building, smaller than a district, with estate governance — is under-represented,
and market participation is again often idealised rather than constrained to real
products [REF].

## 5.6 D6 — DSO transition and local flexibility markets

This stream covers the distribution-system-operator (DSO) transition and the
emergence of local flexibility markets, including the ENA Open Networks products
and platform-procured flexibility [REF]. It has established the market
architecture, procurement volumes, and early liquidity of local flexibility
[FINDING: cite procured MW / £ where available] [REF]. **Park-scale gap:** the
literature analyses the *market side* (products, platforms, DSO processes) largely
from the network's perspective; the *provider side* at estate scale — whether and
how a business park can register, aggregate across MPANs, and stack revenues — is
thinly covered, which is precisely the participation question Section 6 develops
[REF].

## 5.7 D7 — Digital twins for estates

The final stream covers digital twins (DTs) for buildings, campuses, and estates
[REF], establishing a capability ladder from static models to operational,
data-connected twins [FINDING] [REF]. **Park-scale gap:** estate-scale DTs are
mostly demonstrated as technical or operational tools (energy management,
fault detection) rather than as instruments for *market participation and
flexibility valuation*; the link from twin capability to realised flexibility
revenue — the "model → shadow → twin" ladder as a market precondition (Section 7.3)
— is not established [REF].

## 5.8 Synthesis (Table 4)

Read together, the seven streams show a consistent pattern: the *technical* case
for coordinated estate flexibility is well established (D2, D5), the *market*
architecture exists and is maturing (D6), and enabling *governance* models exist in
adjacent domains (D3, D4) — but no stream addresses the UK business park as a
*coordinated, multi-vector, market-participating flexibility provider*. Each
stream's park-scale gap is the row of Table 4; assembled, they define the space
this review claims.
