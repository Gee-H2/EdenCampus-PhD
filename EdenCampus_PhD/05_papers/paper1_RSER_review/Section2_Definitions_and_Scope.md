## 2.1 The absence of a statutory definition

There is no statutory definition of a "business park" in United Kingdom law. The
term is used widely in planning practice, property markets, and policy, but it
denotes a commercial-development typology rather than a legal category [REF]. This
absence is consequential for energy-system analysis: without a definition, business
parks cannot be enumerated, their aggregate demand and flexibility cannot be
bounded, and they cannot be addressed as a participant class in market design. The
definitional gap is therefore not a semantic preliminary but the first substantive
barrier this review must resolve.

## 2.2 Planning-use frameworks across the jurisdictions

The closest legal proxies are the planning use-class systems, which differ across
the UK's jurisdictions and must be handled separately.

**England.** The Town and Country Planning (Use Classes) Order 1987 was
substantially amended in 2020 by the introduction of **Class E ("Commercial,
Business and Service")**, which consolidated former classes covering offices,
research and development, light industrial, retail, and other commercial uses into
a single broad class [VERIFY: Town and Country Planning (Use Classes) (Amendment)
(England) Regulations 2020, effective 1 September 2020]. Class E is significant for
this review because it groups precisely the mixed office/R&D/light-industrial uses
characteristic of business parks, while permitting movement between them without a
change-of-use application [REF].

**Scotland.** Scotland retains a distinct system under the Town and Country
Planning (Use Classes) (Scotland) Order 1997, in which the relevant commercial
categories are **Class 4 (business), Class 5 (general industrial), and Class 6
(storage or distribution)** [VERIFY: Use Classes (Scotland) Order 1997, class
definitions]. Scotland did not adopt the England Class E consolidation, so the
Scottish classes remain more disaggregated — a difference that matters when
comparing estates across the two jurisdictions and when using the Scottish
Assessors valuation roll (Section 4).

**Northern Ireland.** Northern Ireland operates its own Planning (Use Classes)
Order [VERIFY: The Planning (Use Classes) Order (Northern Ireland) 2015 — confirm
year and relevant classes], administered separately and aligned to neither the
England nor Scotland systems.

The divergence across three planning systems is itself a finding: any UK-wide
characterisation of business parks must reconcile three incompatible use-class
taxonomies, which is one reason the asset class has resisted systematic definition.

## 2.3 An operational definition

Because no legal definition serves, this review adopts an explicit operational
definition, applied consistently in the typology (Section 4) and empirical
characterisation. A business park is defined as a development meeting all of:

1. **≥5 separately occupied units** (distinguishing an estate from a single
   large occupier's premises, subject to the T5 single-owner exception noted below);
2. **≥10,000 m² gross internal area (GIA), or ≥2 ha site area** (a scale threshold
   excluding incidental clusters) [VERIFY: justify thresholds against planning /
   property-market conventions];
3. **shared infrastructure** (access, parking, district services, or a common
   landlord/estate framework);
4. **single or coordinated management** (an estate-level governance capable, in
   principle, of coordinated energy decisions).

Criterion 4 is decisive for the flexibility argument: coordinated management is
what allows an estate to act as a single flexibility provider rather than a set of
unrelated meter points. The single-owner corporate campus (Type T5) satisfies
criteria 2–4 through single ownership and is retained despite not meeting the
multi-unit criterion 1, because its governance coherence makes it analytically
relevant.

## 2.4 Boundary cases and exclusions

Several adjacent typologies meet some criteria but are treated as boundary cases:

- **Industrial estates** — included where they meet the criteria (Type T2), though
  the boundary with heavy-industrial sites is a matter of degree [REF].
- **Innovation campuses** — included (Type T3); their anchor-institution governance
  makes them a strong fit for criterion 4.
- **Retail and leisure parks** — *excluded* on governance grounds. They meet the
  physical criteria but their consumer-facing tenant mix and lease structures place
  them within the retail-property literature rather than the coordinated-estate
  flexibility question [REF].
- **Eco-industrial parks and industrial symbiosis sites** — treated as a related
  literature (stream D4, Section 5) rather than a separate type, since their
  defining feature is material/energy exchange rather than governance form [REF].
- **RED II Article 22 renewable energy communities** — used as a *legal comparator*
  rather than a UK type, since the EU renewable-energy-community construct provides
  a governance and market template that GB lacks [VERIFY: RED II (Directive
  (EU) 2018/2001) Article 22 provisions].
