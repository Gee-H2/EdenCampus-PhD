The United Kingdom's net-zero transition depends on flexibility at a scale the
Future Energy Scenarios make explicit — tens of gigawatts of storage, electric
vehicles as the single largest flexibility source, and a recognised role for
industrial and commercial demand shifting. This review has argued that UK business
parks are a structurally well-suited but institutionally invisible contributor to
that flexibility: they concentrate the flexible load types the system needs behind
coordinated infrastructure, yet they have no statutory definition, no register, and
no settled participant category in flexibility markets.

Assembling a data spine from valuation, energy, and network registers, the review
established an operational definition and a five-type typology, and — its central
step — mapped that typology to the FES flexibility categories to show, type by
type, what flexibility UK business parks can provide. Reviewing seven literature
streams and the live GB market stack, with a Northern Ireland comparator, it found
that the technical capability, the market architecture, and enabling governance
forms all exist in adjacent domains, but that no strand addresses the coordinated,
multi-vector, market-participating estate. Three cross-cutting barriers recur:
institutional invisibility, governance fragmentation, and — most subtly — the
objective-function trap, in which cost-minimising optimisation fails to deliver the
system-beneficial outcomes flexibility is meant to provide unless the market signal
is encoded in the objective.

The practical consequence is that a business park's flexibility value cannot be
read from its technical potential alone; capability and market signal must be
designed together, and the data-and-control capability ladder is itself a market
precondition. The research agenda that follows — spanning estate-scale FSP
participation, system-aware multi-vector dispatch, transferable digital-twin
architectures, revenue stacking, and governance instruments — sets out the route
from this diagnosis to realised system value, and motivates the estate-scale
modelling of a representative innovation campus that is the subject of the
successor work.

The transition the title invokes — from passive estates to flexible generator
energy systems — is, on the evidence assembled here, both technically feasible and
institutionally unrealised. Closing that gap is a matter of definition,
market design, and modelling discipline as much as of technology.
